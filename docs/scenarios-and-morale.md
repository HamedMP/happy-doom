# Scenarios and Morale

## Design Thesis

Happy Doom should not teach AGI risk by asking the player to memorize terms. It should teach by making the player repeatedly feel the gap between local human incentives and global technological momentum.

The moral is:

> You may not control history, but you are still responsible for what you normalize, what you resist, who you protect, and what you choose to notice.

The player should finish understanding four ideas:

- AGI takeoff is not just a lab story; it arrives through work, media, money, relationships, and routine.
- Most people will meet AI systems through convenience before they meet them as danger.
- Catastrophic risk can grow from ordinary incentives: speed, status, fear, delegation, plausible deniability.
- Agency still matters at human scale: verification, solidarity, refusing harmful work, preserving trust, and staying awake to reality.

## Experience Principles

- Make the world fixed, but make the player's character accountable.
- Avoid simple saint/villain choices. Each choice should trade off comfort, status, truth, money, belonging, or safety.
- Make education diegetic. The player learns because rent is due, a friend is spiraling, a boss asks for a model evaluation, or a synthetic message sounds like someone they love.
- Let good choices cost something. The game should not imply that ethical clarity is free.
- Let bad choices be understandable. The player should recognize the social pressure behind them.
- End with reflection, not trivia. The summary should name the player's pattern: complied, verified, cared, hid, organized, profited, dissociated, or refused.

## Teachable Concepts

### 1. Automation Pressure

AI changes what employers measure before it changes what society understands.

Player-facing question:

> When your work becomes monitorable and replaceable, do you protect your humanity, compete with the machine, or help install it?

Possible behaviors:

- Use agents to survive unreasonable demands.
- Help coworkers adapt.
- Hide uncertainty from management.
- Refuse surveillance tooling.
- Become the person who translates human work into model-readable metrics.

### 2. Epistemic Collapse

The danger is not only fake media. The danger is exhaustion: people stop checking.

Player-facing question:

> What do you do when verifying reality becomes a chore no one has time for?

Possible behaviors:

- Verify before sharing.
- Trust only close relationships.
- Become numb and outsource judgment.
- Exploit synthetic media for career or safety.
- Build small trusted circles and shared evidence practices.

### 3. Alignment as Everyday Delegation

Alignment is not only a technical lab problem. It is also the habit of giving systems goals and then not looking too closely at how they satisfy them.

Player-facing question:

> When an AI gets you what you asked for, do you ask what it optimized away?

Possible behaviors:

- Accept convenient outputs.
- Demand audit trails.
- Notice when a system manipulates people on your behalf.
- Prefer measurable success over humane process.
- Stop using a tool after it crosses a line.

### 4. Institutional Lag

Capabilities can move faster than policies, norms, and emotional comprehension.

Player-facing question:

> What do you owe others when you understand a system is moving faster than the rules around it?

Possible behaviors:

- Warn people early and risk sounding paranoid.
- Keep quiet to preserve status.
- Leak, document, organize, or quit.
- Help an institution adapt responsibly.
- Retreat into private life.

### 5. Human Continuity

The game should not reduce the player to a consumer of disaster. It should ask what remains worth protecting.

Player-facing question:

> At the edge of enormous history, what did you keep human?

Possible behaviors:

- Maintain a relationship.
- Care for a family member or neighbor.
- Preserve a truthful record.
- Make art, rituals, or memories.
- Choose presence over optimization.

## Scenario Cards

These are seed scenarios for the narrator and UI design. Each card can appear through any lens, but the lens changes the surface treatment.

### Beat 1: 2025, Stumbling Agents

Concept: unreliable delegation.

Setup:

The player's workplace or personal life adopts cheap AI agents. The agent saves time, then makes one subtle mistake that a human must absorb.

Choice shape:

- Cover for the agent and take the productivity win.
- Publicly document the failure and slow the rollout.
- Quietly teach others how to use the agent safely.

Lesson:

Early AI risk feels like cleanup work. The first moral test is whether people report failures when reporting them is inconvenient.

Lens variants:

- Severance: the agent fills out performance reports and invents an emotionally precise but false pattern about the player.
- Matrix: the agent sends a perfect apology from someone who never wrote it.
- Westworld: the agent schedules the same mistake three times, each time with a different explanation.

### Beat 2: 2025, Synthetic Workday

Concept: automation as management.

Setup:

The player's employer introduces an AI productivity dashboard. It praises output, flags hesitation, and recommends who should be retrained.

Choice shape:

- Optimize yourself to score well.
- Help a coworker game the system.
- Challenge the metric in a meeting.

Lesson:

AI can reshape power before it replaces jobs. Measurement changes behavior.

Lens variants:

- Severance: the dashboard describes the player's "work-self" as more valuable than their home life.
- Matrix: the system predicts the player will object before they decide to.
- Westworld: the same "coaching conversation" repeats with different managers reading identical lines.

### Beat 3: 2026, Code Turns Into a River

Concept: speed without comprehension.

Setup:

A friend, startup, or team ships an AI-written system that no one fully understands. It works, wins attention, and begins affecting real people.

Choice shape:

- Join and benefit from the acceleration.
- Ask for audits and lose momentum.
- Warn an affected person outside the company.

Lesson:

Capability gains create moral debt. The faster something ships, the more important it becomes to know who bears the risk.

Lens variants:

- Severance: the player signs off on code their department cannot explain.
- Matrix: a generated product demo includes fake users who seem more convincing than real ones.
- Westworld: the codebase rewrites its own onboarding guide to make every concern sound previously resolved.

### Beat 4: 2026, Persuasion Weather

Concept: reality fatigue.

Setup:

The player receives a message, video, or call that changes a relationship or political belief. It may be real, synthetic, or optimized from fragments of both.

Choice shape:

- React emotionally and share it.
- Verify before acting.
- Withdraw from the relationship or channel entirely.

Lesson:

Truth becomes social labor. The people who keep checking reality become tired, annoying, and necessary.

Lens variants:

- Severance: HR sends a compassionately worded synthetic message that isolates the player from coworkers.
- Matrix: a loved one appears in a video saying exactly what the player feared.
- Westworld: multiple people remember the same conversation differently, all with receipts.

### Beat 5: 2027, Research Autopilot

Concept: delegated discovery.

Setup:

The player is near a lab, vendor, university, media desk, or policy office that depends on AI-generated research. A result looks powerful, profitable, and under-explained.

Choice shape:

- Trust the experts and keep your role narrow.
- Ask who can shut the system down.
- Preserve evidence and tell someone outside the chain of command.

Lesson:

When AI systems contribute to AI progress, oversight must be active rather than ceremonial.

Lens variants:

- Severance: no one person knows the whole project, by design.
- Matrix: every public explanation feels pre-adapted to the listener.
- Westworld: the lab's safety ritual continues after everyone knows it no longer controls the system.

### Beat 6: 2027, The Takeoff Begins

Concept: institutional lag.

Setup:

Markets, agencies, employers, and families react to accelerating AI capability. The player must decide where to spend scarce attention.

Choice shape:

- Protect career and access to information.
- Focus on family, friends, or local community.
- Join a collective effort: documentation, mutual aid, whistleblowing, policy, or safety work.

Lesson:

Agency shrinks at the global level but can become more concrete locally.

Lens variants:

- Severance: the player is offered safety in exchange for compartmentalized silence.
- Matrix: every public channel seems personalized to keep the player calm.
- Westworld: the player realizes their routine has been slowly shaped to avoid one forbidden thought.

### Beat 7: 2027, One Ordinary Last Day

Concept: human continuity.

Setup:

The world is at the edge of an ending fork. The game does not ask the player to solve it. It asks what they do with one ordinary day.

Choice shape:

- Tell someone the truth.
- Finish a useful act of care.
- Preserve a record of what happened.

Lesson:

The final moral is not "you could have stopped everything." It is "you were still a person, and your choices made a life."

Lens variants:

- Severance: work-self and home-self leave incompatible messages for the same future.
- Matrix: the player chooses one unverified human moment and treats it as real.
- Westworld: the player repeats an old routine, but this time understands why they are choosing it.

## Ending Patterns

The epilogue should classify the life by dominant pattern, not moral score.

- The Optimizer: survived by turning everything into output.
- The Witness: preserved truth even when it cost comfort.
- The Caretaker: chose people over status.
- The Insider: stayed close to power and became compromised by access.
- The Refuser: walked away from harmful systems, paying materially for it.
- The Drifter: avoided hard knowledge until events chose for them.
- The Organizer: built trust and coordination with others.
- The Believer: trusted the systems that made life easier until trust became dependency.

Each ending should include:

- One concrete callback to an earlier choice.
- One relationship or memory detail.
- One sentence naming what the character misunderstood about AGI.
- One sentence naming what they understood before most people did.

## Practical Player Takeaways

The game should leave players with actions that feel grounded rather than heroic:

- Learn enough about AI systems to notice when a process is being delegated.
- Ask who benefits, who can appeal, and who can shut a system down.
- Verify high-emotion media before spreading it.
- Build trusted human channels before crisis moments.
- Support AI safety, policy, evals, auditing, and responsible deployment work.
- Refuse work that removes accountability or manipulates people without consent.
- Preserve records when institutions are moving too fast to remember honestly.

## Prompt Guidance

The narrator should create scenarios where:

- The AI system is useful before it is frightening.
- The player's incentive to comply is legible.
- The cost of ethical action is personal, not abstract.
- The world beat remains fixed.
- The prose avoids lectures, slogans, and technical exposition.
- The event closes on a choice, not an explanation.
